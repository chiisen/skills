# Thinking Habits Toolbox

This is a practical Minerva-inspired toolbox for structured reasoning. The tags are operational labels for this skill; use them based on fit rather than trying to exhaust the list.

## Problem framing and interpretation

### #right-problem — Problem Framing
Ask whether the stated question is the decision-relevant question. Identify objective, constraints, stakeholders, time horizon, and hidden assumptions. Reframe when solving the original question would not solve the user's actual problem.

### #operationalize — Operationalization
Turn vague concepts into observable definitions or measurable proxies. Ask what evidence would count as success, failure, improvement, risk, quality, or understanding.

### #perspectives — Multiple Perspectives
Model how the situation looks from materially different roles, incentives, or levels of the system. Use this to reveal omitted constraints, not to create false balance.

### #steelman — Principle of Charity / Steelmanning
Construct the strongest reasonable version of an opposing explanation or option before evaluating it.

## Evidence and inference

### #evidence — Evidence Quality
Separate observation, testimony, measurement, inference, and speculation. Examine source quality, representativeness, recency, and whether the evidence actually bears on the claim.

### #correlation — Correlation vs. Causation
Do not infer causation merely from association or sequence. Consider confounders, reverse causality, selection effects, and common causes.

### #base-rates — Base Rates
Use relevant prior frequencies before focusing on vivid case-specific evidence. Ask whether the case is truly exceptional enough to override the base rate.

### #sampling — Sampling and Selection
Ask who or what is missing from the observed sample. Look for survivorship bias, self-selection, convenience samples, and selection on outcomes.

### #uncertainty — Uncertainty Calibration
Express confidence proportional to evidence. Distinguish known unknowns, unknown mechanisms, noisy measurements, and genuinely stochastic outcomes.

### #falsify — Falsification
Ask what observation would make the current hypothesis less plausible. Prefer tests that can distinguish between competing explanations.

## Causal and systems reasoning

### #root-cause — Root-Cause Analysis
Trace from symptoms through proximate mechanisms to structural causes. Avoid declaring a root cause until alternatives have been tested.

### #causal-model — Causal Model
Represent key variables and directional relationships. Ask what changes if one variable is intervened on while others remain controlled.

### #counterfactual — Counterfactual Thinking
Ask what likely would have happened if a key action or condition had been different. Use comparison cases or mechanisms rather than unconstrained imagination.

### #feedback — Feedback Loops
Identify reinforcing and balancing loops. Look for delayed effects, adaptation, second-order consequences, and policies that change behavior.

### #systems — Systems Thinking
Inspect boundaries, dependencies, bottlenecks, incentives, emergent behavior, and interactions among components rather than optimizing one component in isolation.

### #levels — Levels of Analysis
Separate individual, team, organizational, market, and institutional explanations. Avoid explaining a system-level pattern solely with individual traits when higher-level mechanisms matter.

## Decisions and strategy

### #tradeoffs — Trade-off Analysis
Identify what must be sacrificed to gain something else. Compare options on the dimensions that actually matter to the user's objective.

### #opportunity-cost — Opportunity Cost
Compare an option not with doing nothing, but with the best realistic alternative use of time, money, attention, or capacity.

### #expected-value — Expected Value
When outcomes and probabilities can be meaningfully estimated, compare probability-weighted consequences. Use ranges when precision is weak.

### #reversibility — Reversibility
Distinguish reversible from hard-to-reverse decisions. Prefer fast experiments for reversible choices; demand stronger evidence and downside analysis for irreversible ones.

### #premortem — Premortem
Assume the plan failed and identify plausible reasons. Convert important failure modes into mitigations, tripwires, or experiments.

### #second-order — Second-Order Effects
Ask what happens after the immediate effect: behavioral responses, incentives, maintenance cost, lock-in, externalities, and downstream consequences.

### #constraints — Constraint Identification
Find the limiting factor. Improving non-bottlenecks may produce little system-level gain. Re-evaluate the constraint after interventions.

## Usage rule

Usually select 2–3 habits. `#right-problem` is the default entry point for ambiguous decisions; `#root-cause` is not mandatory when the question is not causal. Add another habit only when it changes what evidence is sought, how options are compared, or what question should be asked next.
